Ancient objects can be bought and owned privately in the United Kingdom. That statement is true, but it is not a sufficient due diligence test.

An antiquity can be genuine and still have a defective legal history. It can have been excavated unlawfully, exported contrary to the law of its source country, stolen from a collection, removed from a protected site or sold by someone who never had good title.

A careful buyer therefore asks about the object's modern history before becoming absorbed by its ancient one.

This is a general overview, not legal advice on a particular object.

## UK law does not create a blanket ban on antiquities

There is no general rule in the United Kingdom that private people may not own Roman, Greek, Egyptian or other ancient objects.

The legal position turns on the facts surrounding the individual object.

One important statute is the Dealing in Cultural Objects (Offences) Act 2003. It creates an offence where a person dishonestly deals in a cultural object that is "tainted", knowing or believing that it is tainted.

The Act defines cultural objects as objects of historical, architectural or archaeological interest. In broad terms, taint can arise where an object was illegally excavated or illegally removed from a building, structure or site after the Act came into force.

The Act is important because it can apply where the unlawful excavation or removal occurred outside the United Kingdom under the law of another country or territory.

That is one reason source-country law cannot be ignored merely because the sale itself takes place in Britain.

## 1970 is an important provenance benchmark, not a universal ownership cut-off

The UNESCO Convention on illicit trade in cultural property was adopted in 1970 and has become an important ethical and due diligence reference point.

The Convention concerns measures taken by states to prevent illicit import, export and transfer of ownership. It does not mean that every antiquity lacking a pre-1970 invoice is automatically illegal to own in the UK.

That would be an inaccurate shortcut.

What 1970 does provide is a widely recognised benchmark for scrutiny. Museums and responsible market participants often ask whether an archaeological object can be shown to have been outside its likely country of origin before 1970, or whether a later export can be demonstrated as lawful.

The British Museum's current acquisitions policy expressly gives weight to provenance, relevant treaties, ethical codes and measures intended to combat illicit trade.

A private collector is not governed by the Museum's acquisition policy, but it is a useful indication of the level of care expected around culturally sensitive material.

## Source-country export law can be decisive

Many countries regulate archaeological material far more tightly than the United Kingdom.

A Roman object from Italy, an Egyptian antiquity, a Mesopotamian tablet or a pre-Columbian object may be subject to patrimony and export rules in the country where it was found.

The correct question is not simply "is it legal to buy this in London?" It is also "how and when did it leave the country whose law applied to it?"

An old collection record can be important because it may place the object outside its source country before later restrictions or before the period now treated as high risk. For later movements, export permits and customs records can be critical.

## UK export rules are a separate issue

Lawful ownership in Britain does not mean an object can automatically be exported from Britain without formalities.

GOV.UK guidance states that a UK export licence is required to export objects of cultural interest from Great Britain to destinations outside the UK, subject to the detailed licensing framework and applicable thresholds and categories.

The licensing system is administered by Arts Council England.

Collectors who intend to move material internationally should therefore treat acquisition and future export as separate questions. An object can be lawfully owned and still require an export licence before it leaves Great Britain.

## Objects found in Britain have their own rules

New finds have their own reporting framework. In England and Wales, objects that meet the statutory definition of treasure must be reported under the Treasure Act 1996. The definition was widened in 2023 to include certain metal objects at least 200 years old that meet a significance test.

The Portable Antiquities Scheme records many archaeological finds made by members of the public, including objects that are not treasure. Its records can provide useful evidence of findspot, identification and reporting history, but PAS recording is not a substitute for the legal duties that apply to treasure.

Scotland and Northern Ireland have different arrangements and should be checked separately.

That is different from buying an object that has already circulated on the established market.

If a seller says a metal-detected object was "found years ago", the date, location, landowner position and any reporting requirements should be explored rather than treated as decorative provenance.

The more recent the discovery, the easier it should usually be to produce a clear record of how the find entered private ownership.

## A dealer's invoice is evidence, not a complete legal opinion

Invoices matter. Reputable auction houses and dealers also carry out their own checks. Neither fact replaces object-specific due diligence.

For a significant antiquity we would normally want to understand the ownership chain, previous publications or sales, old collection labels, export documentation where relevant, the circumstances of excavation or discovery where known, and whether the object appears in stolen-art databases or known loss records.

Gaps should be described as gaps.

A phrase such as "private European collection, acquired before 2000" is only as useful as the evidence behind it. If the underlying invoice, photograph or catalogue cannot be produced, the statement should not be treated as equivalent to documented provenance.

## What a responsible sale should give the buyer

A good catalogue entry should separate what is known from what is inferred.

It should identify the object, state the dating and attribution with appropriate caution, describe restoration, disclose the known ownership history, list supporting documents and explain any relevant export position.

Where the documented provenance begins late, that should be visible.
