Provenance is often reduced to a line beneath an auction lot.

"Private collection, Europe."

"Acquired before 2000."

"Property of a gentleman."

Those statements may be true. On their own, they do not tell a collector very much.

A useful provenance is an evidenced chain of ownership. It should help answer who had the object, when they had it, how it changed hands and, where relevant, how it moved between countries.

## Provenance and archaeological context are not the same thing

The terms are sometimes confused.

Archaeological context describes where an object was found and its relationship to a site, layer, burial, structure or associated material.

Provenance in the collecting market usually describes the object's later ownership history.

An antiquity can have excellent modern provenance and no recorded excavation context. It can also have a known excavation context and a later ownership gap.

Both matter, but they answer different questions.

## A name and a date are only useful if they can be supported

"Collection of Dr X, acquired in the 1960s" is potentially strong provenance.

The next question is what supports it.

Evidence might include an old collection label, a dated photograph, correspondence, an inventory, an insurance schedule, an auction catalogue or an invoice. Better still, several independent documents may agree.

The physical object can also help. Old labels, accession numbers, mounts and collection markings can be matched against archival records.

A recently typed certificate repeating the same story is not independent evidence of the story.

## Auction history is valuable, but read the original catalogue

Earlier auction appearances can create a useful dated anchor.

The strongest record identifies the auction house, sale date and lot number, ideally with a copy of the catalogue page or an archived online record showing the same object.

Do not rely entirely on a later dealer saying "ex Christie's". The original lot may contain qualifications or a different provenance wording that disappeared as the object moved through the market.

Repeated catalogue descriptions can also create the illusion of corroboration when each seller is copying the previous seller.

Follow the chain back to the earliest source you can actually inspect.

## 1970 is a benchmark, not a magic sentence

The UNESCO Convention on illicit trafficking of cultural property was adopted in 1970. That date became an important reference point in museum acquisition policy and market due diligence.

It is common to prefer evidence that an archaeological object was outside its likely country of origin before 1970, or clear proof of lawful export after that date.

But "pre-1970 provenance" still needs evidence.

A vague claim that an object was "believed to have been in a collection since the 1960s" should not be treated like a dated 1965 collection photograph.

Equally, an object first documented after 1970 is not automatically unlawful. Later lawful export can be demonstrated. The underlying national law, export history and facts of the case matter.

## Gaps should remain visible

Real ownership histories are often incomplete.

If an object is documented in a Paris collection in 1958, then disappears from the record until a London sale in 1994, the correct provenance contains a gap.

It should not be smoothed into a continuous sentence unless the intervening ownership is actually known.

This is more than stylistic caution. Future research may fill the gap. A catalogue that preserves uncertainty gives the next owner a more honest starting point.

## Export documents can be more important than famous names

A prestigious former owner adds interest. It does not solve an unlawful export.

Where source-country export restrictions apply, permits and customs records can carry more legal weight than the social standing of a collector.

For objects moving out of Great Britain, UK cultural-goods export licensing may also apply.

A strong dossier therefore combines collecting history with the legal documents relevant to the object's movements.

## Provenance should survive the next sale

The collector has a role in preserving the record.

Keep original labels even if a new display mount is made. Keep invoices and correspondence. Save auction PDFs and web pages before links disappear. Photograph the object with its labels. Record conservation and restoration.

When the object is sold, the documentary archive should travel with it.

The goal is that the next catalogue can say more than the previous one, not less.
