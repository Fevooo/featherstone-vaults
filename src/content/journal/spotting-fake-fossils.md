Fossil forgery is not new. In 1725 colleagues of the Würzburg professor Johann Beringer planted carved limestone "fossils" for him to find, and he published them in good faith. The most famous British case, Piltdown Man, was announced in 1912 and not exposed until 1953, when fluorine dating and anatomical study showed it combined a human skull with an ape's jaw.

What has changed is the scale. Fossils are now traded worldwide, and some are made, assembled or improved for that market. The American Museum of Natural History notes that even university-trained trilobite specialists have been fooled.

Most fakes fall into a few kinds. Knowing them is the best protection.

## Casts and replicas presented as real

The simplest fake is a copy. A mould is taken from a genuine fossil, filled with resin or plaster, coloured, and set into a piece of real rock.

The AMNH's trilobite pages describe North African workshops using everything from local mud to car body filler to make or assemble trilobites. Because the moulds come from real specimens, the anatomy can be right in every detail.

**What we look for.** This is our own practice rather than a published method. A cast is often lighter than it should be and slightly warmer to the touch than stone. Look for tiny bubbles, a seam where a mould joined, a surface texture that is too uniform, and a boundary where the "fossil" meets the rock like an inlay rather than continuing into it. On a genuine specimen, natural cracks usually run through fossil and matrix alike.

## Composites: parts of more than one animal

A composite is assembled from more than one specimen. Sometimes this is done honestly, to complete a skeleton, and declared. Sometimes it is not.

The best-known case is Archaeoraptor, presented in 1999 as a missing link between dinosaurs and birds. When it was studied by X-ray CT, Timothy Rowe and colleagues showed in *Nature* in 2001 that it had been built from many separate pieces of rock. The body was later identified as an early bird and the tail as a small dromaeosaur, *Microraptor*.

Composites are a recurring concern in commercially collected vertebrate material. A 2024 paper in *The Anatomical Record* argued that the teeth of a Moroccan mosasaur specimen had been set into the jaw artificially. A 2025 study using CT scans and a new specimen disputed this. The debate is the point: even specialists with scanners can disagree, which is why a buyer should ask what has been examined, not only what the seller believes.

**What we look for.** Changes in colour or texture between parts that should match, joins hidden under filler, bones or teeth that do not sit naturally in their sockets, and a specimen that is more complete than the material from that locality usually is.

## Painted and enhanced detail

Some forgeries begin with a real fossil and improve it.

In 2024 researchers re-examining *Tridentinosaurus antiquus*, found in the Italian Alps in 1931 and long thought to preserve soft tissue, showed that its dark body outline was paint. The Natural History Museum reported the study. Some of the bones appear to be genuine. The forgery lay in what had been added around them.

Paint, filler and carved detail are used to sharpen a weak specimen, add missing parts or deepen colour. Where this is restoration and disclosed, it is legitimate. Where it is passed off as original, it is fraud.

**What we look for.** Ultraviolet light is the most useful single tool. Many adhesives, resins and fillers fluoresce differently from fossil and rock, so repairs that cannot be seen in normal light often show clearly under UV. Our [note on museum-quality fossils](/journal/museum-quality-fossil) explains how preparation and restoration should be described.

## Amber and copal

Insects in amber are among the most forged fossils of all.

Fakes range from modern resin with insects set into it, to genuine amber that has been opened, given an insect and resealed. The Gemological Institute of America has described a piece with a natural Baltic amber core inside a shell of polymer resin, holding one natural insect and one placed artificially. Under ultraviolet light the amber and the resin fluoresced in different colours, and infrared spectroscopy confirmed which was which.

Young, hardened tree resin, called copal, is also sold as amber. The Mineralogical Society of America notes that amber, copal and some plastics all float in salt water, so the familiar brine test cannot separate them, and that solvent tests can mark a polished surface.

**What we look for.** Insects that are too perfect, too large or too neatly placed; clusters of bubbles around the inclusion; and a seller who cannot say where the piece came from. Hot-needle and solvent tests damage the piece, so we leave them to a laboratory on anything worth testing.

## Before you buy

A genuine fossil sold honestly can withstand questions. Ask:

- Where was it found, and how is that known?
- Who prepared it, and what was done?
- Is any part restored, cast, reconstructed or taken from another specimen?
- Has it been examined under UV light, and will the seller show you?
- For an expensive piece, has it been CT scanned, or can it be?

Expect plain answers, including "we do not know". A seller who is vague about restoration is telling you something.
