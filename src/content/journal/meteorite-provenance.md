A meteorite can be scientifically well classified and commercially poorly documented at the same time.

That distinction is easy to miss. The Meteoritical Bulletin Database records recognised meteorites, their approved names, classifications, initial descriptions and type-specimen repositories. It does not certify every fragment later offered under that name.

For a collector, classification answers "what meteorite is this recognised material from?" Provenance answers the additional question, "why should we believe this particular specimen is part of it?"

## Start with the recognised meteorite, not the seller's label

The Meteoritical Bulletin Database is the primary reference point for officially recognised meteorite names.

A good catalogue entry should use the approved name accurately and should not invent a more marketable version of it. Dense collection areas, especially in North West Africa, make exact naming and pairing particularly important because visually similar material can have very different analytical histories.

The database can tell you whether a name is approved, what classification has been published and where the type material is held. It can also show the original find circumstances and total known weight when those details were reported.

Those are scientific records. They are the beginning of private-specimen due diligence rather than the end.

## The chain from the main mass matters

Imagine a classified meteorite with a well documented type specimen in a university collection. A dealer later offers a 12 gram slice carrying the same name.

The scientific record proves the meteorite exists. It does not by itself prove that the 12 gram slice was cut from the recognised material.

That connection is usually established through a chain of custody. It may run from the finder to the classifier, from the main mass to a recognised dealer or preparator, and from there to the current collection. Old labels, specimen cards, invoices, collection numbers and photographs can all contribute.

For major historic falls and famous pallasites, earlier museum or collection documentation can be particularly valuable.

## Classification and provenance should agree with the specimen

The physical specimen still has to make sense.

A polished pallasite slice should be consistent with the published mineralogy of the named meteorite. An ordinary chondrite should not be marketed as a rare planetary specimen merely because a handwritten label says so. A piece sold as a witnessed fall should have a recovery history compatible with the documented fall.

Laboratory testing can answer some questions, but even analysis has limits. Meteorites can be paired with other finds from the same strewn field or dense collection area. A laboratory result may establish a classification while leaving the exact approved name unresolved.

This is one reason an old, specific chain of custody can carry value beyond a fresh analytical certificate.

## "Lunar" and "Martian" deserve especially careful wording

Lunar and Martian meteorites are identified through scientific characteristics that link them to material from those planetary bodies. They are not samples returned by Apollo astronauts or Mars missions.

That distinction should never be blurred.

A lunar meteorite can legitimately be privately owned when it has been lawfully recovered and traded. Apollo lunar samples have a completely different ownership history. US legislation confirming astronaut ownership of certain space artefacts expressly excludes lunar rocks and other lunar material.

For collectors crossing between natural history and spaceflight, the words "from the Moon" therefore describe two very different provenance systems.

## A useful specimen record is specific

At minimum, we want a meteorite record to identify the approved name, classification, specimen mass and the person or collection from which the piece was obtained.

For a cut specimen, it is useful to know who prepared it and, where practical, what larger mass it came from.

For high value pieces, photographs of the specimen at earlier stages in its ownership can be extremely helpful. Labels should be retained even when a newer catalogue card is produced.

For witnessed falls, the date and documented recovery story matter. For historic finds, earlier collection history can become part of the scientific and cultural importance of the object.

## Provenance can change value without changing geology

Two slices from the same meteorite can be geologically equivalent and still be very different collecting objects.

One may have descended from a named nineteenth-century collection, carry an old museum-style label and appear in a published catalogue. Another may have no history before a recent online sale.

The mineralogy may be the same. The documentary depth is not.

That is why we catalogue meteorites as specimens with histories rather than simply as pieces of extraterrestrial rock. The scientific name tells us what the material is. Provenance tells us what this piece is.
